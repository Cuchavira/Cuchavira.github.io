TITLE

Touch-to-Meaning: Mapping Neurodivergent Learners’ Tactile Experiences into Semantic Vocabulary for Future Adaptive Tangible AI

Short working title: Touch-to-Meaning

Current status

Overall for project

Touch-to-Meaning investigates how neurodivergent learners express tactile/haptic experience during hands-on learning, especially when visible actions such as repeating, avoiding, pausing, pressing, or requesting change cannot be read as direct evidence of preference or comfort. The project aims to build traceable ways to connect tactile episodes, learner expression, educator interpretation, and future adaptive tangible design.

CHI 2027 proposal direction

This project is being developed as a CHI 2027 study proposal. The planned contribution is a formative HCI study and early framework, not a finished system paper. The proposal will position tactile/haptic experience as a communication and interpretation problem in neurodivergent learning: how can a classroom activity make tactile meaning visible enough to discuss, annotate, and later use in adaptive tangible design?

Core question for the project

When neurodivergent learners work with tactile materials in a classroom learning activity, how can their tactile responses be observed, expressed, and interpreted without reducing those responses to behavior traces alone?

Research goals

Research goal: develop a grounded account of how tactile experience becomes meaningful in classroom interaction, then use that account to inform educator interpretation and future adaptive tangible design.

The study will:

  1. describe how tactile episodes unfold across material contact, body movement, visual scaffolding, control, and social facilitation;
  2. identify how learners express comfort, intensity, effort, predictability, agency, refusal, repetition, and preference;
  3. adapt micro-phenomenology through replay, artifact-anchored prompts, tokens, gesture, drawing, AAC, and educator-supported interpretation;
  4. explore icon-only physical controls for stop, pause, repeat, less, change, help, and done;
  5. translate tactile episodes into source-aware, uncertainty-aware, overrideable records for future adaptive tangible systems.

Unresolved planning note: this goal list needs to be reconfigured later. Before continuing this section, ask how these study aims should be revised and what claims the proposal should prioritize.

Research questions

These research questions move from observing tactile response patterns, to translating multimodal expression, to tracing educator interpretation, and then building a framework.

  1. What tactile response patterns emerge when neurodivergent learners engage with tactile materials in a structured learning activity?

  2. How can learners’ multimodal expressions around tactile episodes be translated into traceable semantic descriptions of tactile experience?

  3. What educator knowledge is needed to interpret and respond to learners’ tactile expressions?

  4. How salient and stable are the semantic descriptions of tactile experience across multiple sessions?

Working status: RQ4 remains provisional because salience and stability require repeated evidence across multiple sessions. RQ3 is now focused on educator knowledge for interpreting and responding to learners' tactile expressions.

Overall method

Method: staged formative study using a tactile-rich classroom activity, educator pilot workshop, learner sessions, replay-supported elicitation, and educator debrief.

The study first works with educators to identify likely tactile response patterns and adapt the partner school’s mathematics-art lesson into a research-ready activity. Learner sessions then use the activity to create observable tactile episodes, which are revisited through multimodal expression such as object choice, gesture, drawing, AAC, reenactment, or short prompts. Analysis connects episode records, learner expression, and educator interpretation while preserving uncertainty and source labels.

Initial setup

Initial setup is the preparation package for the June design probe. It should not repeat the full study design. This section records what must be ready before the educator workshop and pilot sessions.

Setup scope:

  • Curriculum anchor: use the Week 9-14 mathematics-art topics as the first activity space, with the tactile clock task as the initial walkthrough.
  • Probe kit: prepare varied tactile modules, simple attachable mechanics, teacher-managed visual route cards, and icon-only control tokens.
  • Support surface: use a tabletop mat, tray, or classroom panel only as a stable workspace; the board itself is not the research focus.
  • Observation tools: prepare two sheets, one lightweight teacher sheet for classroom noticing and one researcher sheet for episode-level traceability.
  • Data capture plan: decide whether each session uses written notes only, notes plus photos, or notes plus short video, based on school and ethics approval.
  • Safety and access check: remove materials that are too small, sharp, fragile, noisy, hard to clean, or difficult for learners to handle.
  • Research boundary: do not build live AI adaptation, full computer vision tracking, an automated route system, or a teacher dashboard for the June study.

Setup output: a classroom-ready low-fidelity probe kit, printed observation sheets, a short task walkthrough, and a protocol skeleton. The detailed rationale and workshop plan are kept in plan/touch_to_meaning_initial_setup_component_rationale_2026-05-25.md.

Recruiting

Recruitment and study access are organized through an existing collaboration with a local special education school.

Planned participants:

  • around 12 neurodivergent children from the school program;
  • 3 educators from the partner school.

This phase does not recruit caregivers, therapists, or additional external stakeholders. The educator role is limited to classroom knowledge, curriculum alignment, practical facilitation, and interpretation of learner responses within the school context.

Learner descriptions will use design-relevant profiles: sensory seeking or avoidance, tactile sensitivity, communication mode, attention and pacing needs, fine-motor access, gross-motor comfort, predictability needs, and support routines.

Study design

Study design: staged formative study anchored in the partner school’s mathematics-art curriculum.

The study design is currently an outline. Learner-session protocol, educator debrief prompts, consent/assent process, and analysis workflow still need to be specified after the one-week design probe workshop.

One-week design probe workshop

The first workshop week focuses mainly on RQ1: how to gather knowledge about learners’ tactile response patterns and tactile preferences before the in-situ formative study. The workshop uses educators’ classroom knowledge and a low-fidelity task walkthrough to decide what should be observed later with learners.

The lo-fi probe contains four priority components:

  • varied tactile modules;
  • simple attachable mechanics;
  • visual route cues connected to time and clock learning;
  • tangible icon-only control tokens.

The board is treated only as a support surface. The walkthrough scenario is a tactile clock task: make and read a tactile clock for 3 o’clock. The learner-facing path is: choose a tactile material, place the number 3, place or check other clock numbers, move the clock hand to 3 o’clock, add a tactile decoration or texture, and use a token to stop, pause, repeat, change, ask for help, or finish.

Workshop outputs should include a tactile module inventory, educator tactile-preference map, seed RQ1 episode codebook, clock-task walkthrough script, teacher observation sheet v1, researcher observation sheet v1, and protocol update for the later in-situ formative study.

The two observation sheets are necessary for this study: the teacher sheet should stay light enough for classroom use, while the researcher sheet should preserve episode timing, material properties, action codes, token use, support moves, and unresolved interpretation for later RQ1 analysis.

Curriculum anchor

Sources: original PDF materials/26年春季数学美社教学计划 最新版.pdf; English translation materials/2026_spring_math_art_teaching_plan_en.md.

The collaborator’s spring mathematics-art curriculum provides the learning context for the tactile activity. The broader curriculum combines number sense, quantity, time, comparison, pattern, symmetry, and art-making. For this study, Weeks 9-14 are the most relevant anchor because they shift from number composition into time and comparison:

Weeks Learning concept Classroom task Possible tactile medium
Week 9 Clock structure and time awareness Recognize clocks; identify numbers 1-12, hour hand, and minute hand; make a watermelon clock. Tactile clock face, placeable numbers, rotating hands, seed/stripe decorations.
Week 10 Reading whole hours Identify and read whole-hour times; connect time to daily life. Movable clock hands, time cards, picture-time matching pieces.
Week 11 Daily schedule and temporal order Recognize day/night and morning/noon/afternoon; plan a daily schedule. Sequencing cards, tactile day-plan board, activity icons.
Weeks 12-14 Comparison and quantity relations Compare numbers within 5, 10, and 20 using greater-than, less-than, and equal signs. Tactile comparison signs, countable objects, sorting trays, crocodile/butterfly/fruit comparison crafts.

These curriculum topics can create structured tactile episodes without locking the study to one prototype. The likely first focus remains the Week 9-14 sequence because it provides repeated opportunities for placing, rotating, sorting, comparing, sequencing, and revising tactile objects.
